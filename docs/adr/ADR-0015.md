# ADR-0015: Delayed delivery — scheduling, snapshot storage and execution

Status: **Accepted** — 2026-08-01 (Prompt 7)

Supersedes nothing. Implements [ADR-0007](ADR-0007.md), which has been frozen since
Prompt 1A and never built.

## Context

ADR-0007 settled the **policy** of a delayed delivery: snapshot at scheduling
time, cancel on disable or delete, re-validate at execution, resolve placeholders
fresh, never re-arm on edit. It deliberately settled no **implementation**: it
does not say where the snapshot lives, when the identity is claimed, what the
scheduler contract is, or what each re-validation failure records.

Those are the decisions that make the difference between a delayed email and a
lost one, so they are written down here before any of them is coded.

Everything below is separate-mode only. [ADR-0013](ADR-0013.md) §2 already
**refuses** a non-zero delay on an insert rule at the repository boundary, and
that refusal stands: insert content is injected into an email WooCommerce is
already composing, so there is no later moment at which delayed content could
reach it. A delayed insert rule is not a feature that is missing — it is a
combination that cannot mean anything. Verified still refused, and tested.

## Decision

### 1. The identity is claimed at SCHEDULING time, not at execution

When a delayed rule matches, the delivery is claimed immediately with
`final_status = 'scheduled'`, and only then is the job queued.

**Why not claim at execution.** ADR-0004's atomic claim is the single mechanism
preventing a duplicate send, and [ADR-0012](ADR-0012.md) §7 already records that
the scheduler's own pre-check is best-effort — two requests can both pass it. If
the claim happened at execution, two queued jobs would both reach the claim and
one would be suppressed, which is fine; but the *trigger* firing twice before
either job ran would queue two jobs, and nothing in between would have recorded
that a delivery was already owed. The claim is what makes the second trigger a
no-op, and it has to happen when the second trigger arrives.

**The identity is unchanged by delay.** `order | rule | separate | trigger_identity`,
exactly as for an immediate delivery. ADR-0004 is explicit that delay is not part
of the key, and it must not become part of it: a merchant who changes a rule's
delay from 1 hour to 2 must not thereby give an already-delivered order a second
email.

#### 1a. ⚠ A CANCELLED DELAYED DELIVERY HAS CONSUMED ITS IDENTITY

State this plainly, because it reads like a bug and is not one.

A delivery that is scheduled and then cancelled — because the rule was disabled,
or the order was refunded — **keeps its tombstone**. That rule cannot fire again
for that order and that trigger identity, ever, even if the merchant re-enables
the rule the next minute.

That is ADR-0004's once-per-order-per-rule-per-identity rule working exactly as
designed, and the alternative is worse: releasing the identity on cancellation
would mean a rule toggled off and on re-delivered to every order still inside its
delay window. Manual resend through the linked attempt rows remains available, and
that is the intended remedy.

**Nobody may read a cancellation as a retry opportunity.** The `cancelled`
tombstone is a terminal state with a reason attached, not a slot waiting to be
refilled.

### 2. The snapshot lives on the DURABLE tombstone, and is released at a terminal state

ADR-0007 says "the claim row snapshots". The claim row is the **tombstone**, and
that turns out to be the only place it can live.

**Why not the detail row, which already has a `snapshot` column.** Two independent
mechanisms would destroy it:

| Mechanism | What it would do |
|---|---|
| `DeliveryDetailRepository::purge_older_than()` | a `scheduled` row falls in the "everything else" tier and is **deleted** at the normal retention window. A delay longer than that window loses its own content. |
| `DeliveryDetailRepository::anonymize()` | `snapshot` is a member of `PERSONAL_FIELDS`, so a privacy erasure request **NULLs it** — leaving a queued job with nothing to send. |

Both are correct behaviours for the store they act on. The detail store is the
**purgeable, personal** half of ADR-0009's split; the snapshot is neither history
nor personal data — it is **pending work**. Putting pending work in the purgeable
store is a category error, and the two mechanisms above are what that error looks
like in production.

So: a new `snapshot longtext NULL` column on the `deliveries` table. Schema v1 is
unreleased, so this is an **in-place amendment of v1 and NOT migration 2**, the
same treatment `insert_lookup` received in Prompt 5.

**⚠ AND IT IS RELEASED WHEN THE DELIVERY REACHES A TERMINAL STATE.** Tombstones are
never purged, so a snapshot left behind would accumulate one rule body per
delivery, for the lifetime of the store — a resource growing without bound in
normal operation. The snapshot is needed only between scheduling and execution, so
`set_final_status()` clears it on the way to **every** terminal state —
`sent`, `failed`, `cancelled`, `skipped`, `unresolved`, `abandoned`
(`DeliveryRepository::SNAPSHOT_RELEASING_STATUSES`). Durable storage is therefore
bounded by the **queue depth**, not by delivery history.

`scheduled` is pointedly absent from that list — it is the state the snapshot
exists for — and so is `claimed`, which an immediate delivery passes through and
which a scheduled one must never be walked back into. **`executing` joined them in
Prompt 7A** (§8.2): a delivery under lease is still pending work, and one whose
lease is later swept has to know what it was going to send.

`DeliveryRepository::transition()` releases the snapshot on the same list and by
the same rule, so a delivery that reaches its terminal state through the guarded
path is bounded exactly as one that reaches it through `set_final_status()`.

**The `scheduled` DETAIL row is a different thing and is purgeable as normal.** It
records when the message is due and which action carries it; retention may remove
it after the usual window, and a delay longer than that window still executes
correctly, because the row that matters is the tombstone. That separation is the
whole point of §2.

#### 2a. What the snapshot contains, and what it must never contain

Per ADR-0007, and no more:

| Field | Why |
|---|---|
| `revision` | the rule version that produced this delivery, recorded on send |
| `subject`, `heading`, `content` | the **templates**, unrendered |
| `recipients` | the recipient **definitions** — tokens and literals, not resolved addresses |
| `matched_items` | line-item ids, for the §4 refund check and for placeholder scoping |
| `mode`, `trigger_identity` | so the executing job claims and sends as the same delivery |
| `delay_seconds`, `scheduled_for` | the delay that produced the scheduled time, for the audit and for the §4 phase check |

**NO RENDERED PERSONAL DATA.** A snapshot is a rule state, not a message. Nothing
resolved from the order — no name, no address, no total, no email address — is
stored. This is what makes the durable half of the split honest: tombstones hold
no personal data, which is precisely why they are never purged and never erased.

**One consequence must be recorded rather than glossed.** Recipient *definitions*
may contain a literal address a merchant typed into the rule (`warehouse@example.test`).
That is merchant configuration, already stored in the clear in the rules table, so
snapshotting it creates no new class of exposure — but the privacy exporter's
tombstone item currently states the row "contains no direct contact or
message-content fields", and for a **pending** delayed delivery that sentence
would be false. The wording is corrected rather than left to drift, and the
snapshot is not added to the export: it is the merchant's rule content, not the
data subject's.

### 3. Execution: snapshot for content, live order for values

At execution, in this order:

1. **content** comes from the snapshot — subject, heading and body templates as
   they were when the delivery was scheduled;
2. **recipients** resolve fresh from the snapshotted **definitions**, so a
   `customer` token picks up the order's current billing address;
3. **placeholders** resolve fresh against the **live order** ([ADR-0014](ADR-0014.md)
   §8 already requires send-time resolution, and this is the same rule reaching its
   most extreme case);
4. the delivery records the **snapshotted revision**, because that is the rule
   version that produced the message.

Editing a rule never mutates or re-arms a queued or past delivery. Adding a rule
after scheduling never joins a queued delivery: the job carries one rule id and
re-fetches only that rule.

### 4. Execution-time re-validation — every check, in order, each with its own reason

Before anything is sent. **The snapshot supplies CONTENT; the re-fetch supplies
PERMISSION** — [ADR-0011](ADR-0011.md) §7a's freshness contract, discharged the
same way the ADR-0008 deferred job discharges it.

| # | Check | On failure |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | the rule still exists | `cancelled` — `rule_deleted` |
| 2 | the rule is still enabled | `cancelled` — `rule_disabled` |
| 3 | the rule is still in this phase (`separate`, delay unchanged, `consolidation` unchanged¹) | `cancelled` — `rule_left_phase` |
| 3a | the rule's `consolidation` is still **inside the vocabulary**² | `cancelled` — `consolidation_invalid` |
| 4 | the order still exists **and is not in the trash** | `cancelled` — `order_deleted` |
| 5 | the order is not cancelled, failed or fully refunded | `cancelled` — `order_state` |
| 6 | at least one snapshotted matched item still exists and is not fully refunded | `cancelled` — `items_refunded` |

> ² ⚠ **ADDED by [ADR-0016](ADR-0016.md) §1a (Prompt 8A).** Check 3 compares the live
> value against the snapshot, which catches a CHANGE; this catches CORRUPTION, and the
> two need separate reasons because they need separate remedies. A rule holding
> `daily` — legitimately storable before Prompt 8 — must not deliver at execution any
> more than it may at trigger time, and `rule_left_phase` would tell the merchant they
> had edited something when what they actually have is a row from an older vocabulary.
> It is checked **before** check 3 so the distinct reason wins; check 3's own
> validity test is consequently unreachable and kept only as depth.
>
> ¹ ⚠ **AMENDED by [ADR-0016](ADR-0016.md) §8.** Check 3 read `consolidation = none`
> while consolidation was unimplemented behaviour, so acquiring any value took the
> rule out of every phase. Prompt 8 implements it, so a delayed `per_product` rule is
> one this phase OWNS and what matters is whether the merchant CHANGED it: the live
> value must equal the SNAPSHOTTED one — the same treatment the delay already gets,
> and for the same reason. Switching a queued delivery between one message and one per
> product changes how many emails it would send, so the queued message is no longer
> the one the merchant asked for. The snapshot carries `consolidation` from v2 onward
> to make the comparison possible.

**Every cancellation finalises the tombstone `cancelled` with a DISTINCT, TRUTHFUL
reason.** One generic "cancelled" would make the log unable to answer the only
question anyone asks of it — *why didn't this send?* — and the six causes have six
different remedies.

**⚠ CHECK 4 IS TWO QUESTIONS, AND WOOCOMMERCE MAKES THEM DIFFERENT.**
`wc_get_order()` **returns a trashed order** — the trash is a soft delete, so the
object comes back intact with status `trash`. "The order still exists" is
therefore not the same question as "the merchant still has this order", and a
check that asked only the first sent a delayed email about an order the merchant
had deleted. Found by this ADR's own check-4 test. A trashed order cancels, under
`order_deleted` rather than `order_state`, because deleting is what the merchant
actually did — and restoring from the trash does not un-send an email.

Check 3 is not redundant with checks 1 and 2. A merchant who switches a rule from
`separate` to `insert`, or who changes its delay, has changed what the rule *is*;
delivering the queued message would deliver a rule that no longer exists in that
form. Cancelling is the only truthful answer, and it keeps the phase-isolation
guarantee ([ADR-0012](ADR-0012.md) §9) intact across time as well as across a
single request.

**Order of the checks is deliberate**: rule checks are one indexed read and settle
the commonest cases (a merchant disabling a rule); order checks come next; the
per-item refund walk is last because it is the most expensive.

### 5. Cancellation from the other direction, and why both directions exist

Disabling or deleting a rule **immediately** cancels its pending scheduled
deliveries — the tombstones are finalised `cancelled` and the queued actions are
unscheduled. That is ADR-0007's wording ("immediately cancels") taken literally,
and it is not merely an optimisation: a merchant who disables a rule expects the
mail to stop, and a job that stays in the queue for another six hours is a
promise this plugin has not kept, however correctly it later declines to send.

**The execution-time re-validation in §4 remains, as the backstop.** Two paths, on
purpose:

- eager cancellation cannot be complete — a rule row deleted directly in SQL, a
  migration, a staging-database restore, or a failure part-way through the eager
  sweep all leave jobs behind;
- execution-time re-validation cannot be timely — it runs when the job runs, which
  is exactly the delay the merchant wanted to stop.

Neither is sufficient; each covers the other's gap. A design with only one of them
is a design that either sends mail a merchant switched off, or leaves queue
entries nobody can account for.

Deactivation unschedules pending delivery jobs, as Prompt 4A item 6 established
for deferred evaluation. Uninstall likewise.

**⚠ THAT IS NO LONGER THE WHOLE OF IT — see §8.6.** Unscheduling alone left every
pending tombstone `scheduled` for ever: the delivery lost, its identity still
consumed, and its snapshot with no clearing path. Both lifecycle paths now
**finalise the tombstone first and unschedule second**, and uninstall does so on
the retain-data path too, which is the default.

### 6. Action Scheduler — the two verified traps, and the honest guarantee

This is the first **real** timing-dependent integration; the POC simulated the
queue. Two facts established in earlier phases are load-bearing:

- **⚠ `$unique = true` IGNORES THE ARGUMENT SET.**
  `ActionScheduler_DBStore::build_where_clause_for_insert()` filters on
  `status IN (pending, running) AND hook = %s AND group_id = %d` with **no args
  term**, verified against the bundled copy and confirmed by experiment in Prompt
  4A. Passing it would mean that while any delayed delivery is pending, **every
  other order's is silently refused**. It is not passed. The args-exact
  `as_has_scheduled_action()` pre-check keeps the queue tidy; **the atomic ADR-0004
  claim is what prevents a duplicate send**, and it is the only thing that does.
- **⚠ `as_unschedule_all_actions( $hook, array(), $group )` MATCHES ONLY
  EXACTLY-EMPTY ARGS.** Cancelling a hook's real actions requires `null`.

Scheduling outcomes are the same four `DeferredEvaluation` established — `scheduled`,
`already_pending`, `scheduler_unavailable`, `schedule_failed` — with the action id
captured. **A scheduling failure is recorded loudly**: a delivery that silently
never scheduled is a lost email, and the one thing it must never be mistaken for
is a duplicate that was correctly suppressed.

Action Scheduler is verified **initialised**, not merely loaded: the functions load
before the data store does, and scheduling against an uninitialised store returns 0.

Jobs are scheduled at `time() + delay_seconds`, UTC, consistent with the UTC
storage established in Prompt 2A.

### 7. Phase isolation: `delay_seconds` moves to implemented

`Orchestrator::UNIMPLEMENTED_BEHAVIOUR_DEFAULTS` loses `delay_seconds` and keeps
`consolidation` as its only remaining entry.

> ⚠ **SUPERSEDED by [ADR-0016](ADR-0016.md) §9: the enumeration is now EMPTY.**
> `consolidation` was its last entry and Prompt 8 implements it, so a
> `per_product` rule belongs to whichever phase its mode and delay put it in — one
> job for a delayed one, fanning out at execution (ADR-0016 §8) — and the vocabulary
> is enforced at the write boundary instead, where an unrecognised value cannot be
> stored at all. **The constant and its test survive** and the test asserts
> emptiness, for exactly the reason the paragraph below gives: dropping the mechanism
> because it currently holds nothing would drop the thing that caught
> `consolidation`.

This is an **explicit, tested change**, not a silently weakened assertion: the
enumeration test asserts the new list exactly, so a future column added to the
enumeration — or removed from it — has to be written down by whoever changed it.
That discipline is why `consolidation` was caught in Prompt 5C, and dropping it
here would be dropping the thing that caught it.

The immediate phase now admits `separate` + `delay_seconds = 0`; the scheduled
phase admits `separate` + `delay_seconds > 0`; insert mode admits `insert` +
`delay_seconds = 0` (§ above). The three are disjoint by construction and the
disjointness is asserted.

### 8. THE SCHEDULED STATE MACHINE (added Prompt 7A)

Sections 1 to 7 settled *what* a delayed delivery is. They did not settle *how a
row moves between the states*, and that omission was one defect wearing six
faces:

| Face | What it looked like |
|---|---|
| `set_final_status()` is an unconditional primary-key `UPDATE` | a cancellation could overwrite `sent`; a send could overwrite `cancelled` |
| no lease between reading `scheduled` and writing the outcome | a worker that timed out **after `wp_mail()` succeeded** left the action re-claimable, and the next worker **sent the customer a second email** |
| `Deactivator` unschedules but never finalises | the delivery is lost, its identity stays consumed, and the snapshot has no clearing path |
| `uninstall.php` returned before its Action Scheduler cleanup | the **default** uninstall path left queued actions whose hook no longer exists |
| `run()` had `return;` branches that exited with the job consumed | notably `! Migrator::is_operational()`, which fires during **any plugin update** |
| post-claim scheduling had no `Throwable` boundary | a throw escaped into `woocommerce_order_status_changed`, leaving `scheduled` with no job |

The cause behind all six: **the `scheduled` state had no atomic transitions and
no guaranteed terminal exit.** One state machine replaces six patches.

```
                 ┌─────────────┐
   claim ───────►│  scheduled  │
                 └──────┬──────┘
        lease ──────────┤────────── cancel
                        ▼                ▼
                 ┌─────────────┐   ┌───────────┐
                 │  executing  │   │ cancelled │  (terminal)
                 └──────┬──────┘   └───────────┘
                        ▼
    ┌─────────┬─────────┬──────────────┬───────────┬───────────┐
    │  sent   │ failed  │  unresolved  │ cancelled │  skipped  │  (terminal)
    └─────────┴─────────┴──────────────┴───────────┴───────────┘
```

#### 8.1 Every transition is conditional on its source state

`DeliveryRepository::transition( $delivery_id, $from, $to, $revision = 0 )` is a
single `UPDATE … WHERE id = %d AND final_status = %s` and returns true only when
**exactly one row changed**.

**Whoever wins the transition owns the outcome; the loser exits without acting.**
That sentence is the whole concurrency design, and it is why the return value may
never be ignored.

Reading the outcome from the affected-row count is safe here for the same reason
it is safe in `claim()`: WordPress does not set `CLIENT_FOUND_ROWS`, so MySQL
reports rows **changed**, and every permitted transition changes `final_status`
to a different value. A no-op cannot be mistaken for a win, and `WHERE id = %d`
on the primary key means the count is never above 1.

`set_final_status()` keeps its place for the **immediate** path, where the
tombstone goes `claimed → terminal` inside one request with no second actor.
**No scheduled path may use it**, and the scheduled paths thread a required
`$from` down through `DeliveryLogger` so that "which state am I leaving" is a
thing every caller has to answer rather than a thing it can forget.

**The permitted transitions, and nothing else:**

| From | To | Why it exists |
|---|---|---|
| `scheduled` | `executing` | §8.2 — the lease |
| `scheduled` | `cancelled` | eager cancellation (§5), a scheduling failure (§6), deactivation and uninstall (§8.6) |
| `executing` | `sent` | the mailer reported success |
| `executing` | `failed` | the mailer reported failure, or the run threw |
| `executing` | `unresolved` | the stale-lease sweep (§8.3) — the outcome was never learned |
| `executing` | `cancelled` | §4's six re-validation checks, plus §8.4's distinct refusals |
| `executing` | `skipped` | ADR-0012 §2's claiming skips, reached during the send |

**⚠ THE LAST TWO EDGES ARE NOT OPTIONAL, AND THEY ARE WORTH STATING PLAINLY
BECAUSE THE FIVE-EDGE VERSION OF THIS TABLE LOOKS COMPLETE.** §4 mandates
`cancelled` with a distinct reason for all six re-validation outcomes, and those
checks run **after** the lease is taken (§8.2) — so without `executing →
cancelled` either §4 would have to be weakened to `failed`, which would be untrue
(nothing failed; the merchant changed their mind), or the lease would have to be
taken after the checks, which would widen the duplicate-email window §8.2 exists
to close. Likewise ADR-0012 §2's `skipped` — no deliverable recipient, the
per-delivery `woocommerce_email_enabled_{id}` filter refusing — is discovered
inside the send, under the lease. A transition table that omits them is a table
that forces a lie into the log.

#### 8.1a A guarded write reports a FACT, not a boolean (added Prompt 7B)

§8.1 said the return value "may never be ignored". It was never ignored — it was
**merged**. `transition()` and `arm_scheduled()` both returned `bool`, and `false`
meant two different things that need **opposite** handling:

| Fact | What it means | Correct action |
|---|---|---|
| `changed` | this caller moved the row | own the outcome |
| `lost_race` | the statement ran, matched nothing | **exit** — the delivery has an owner |
| `query_failed` | the statement failed | **do not exit** — nobody owns it |
| `refused` | never attempted (bad id, pair outside the table) | as `query_failed` |

`Domain\WriteResult` carries the distinction. `is_shortfall()` is the predicate
every call site that used to read `false` should be asking: *a lost race is
somebody else's success, and a shortfall is nobody's.*

**Three call sites acted on the ambiguity, and all three lost a delivery with no
exception anywhere** — every containment boundary in this plugin catches a
`Throwable`, and a returned `false` produces none:

1. **The arm.** `queue_contained()` treated a non-arm as an inert skip. A plain
   database failure therefore left the identity claimed, the tombstone `claimed`,
   no job, no detail row and nothing for the §8.3 sweep to find — it looks for
   `scheduled` rows — while every later trigger for that identity stayed
   suppressed. The recovery added for a *throw* before arming (§8.5) does not
   cover a returned failure.
2. **The lease.** `run()` read the row back and exited for every existing row, on
   the stated reasoning that it is "already terminal or owned". That is true of a
   statement that RAN and matched nothing. It is false of one that FAILED: the row
   is then still `scheduled`, and the action is consumed by returning normally.
3. **The cancellation.** `ScheduledCancellation` unscheduled the job and inspected
   the result *afterwards*, under a comment claiming the job was removed only once
   the tombstone was terminal.

**What each does now:**

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| arm failed, row still `claimed` | terminalise, reason `arm_failed` |
| arm failed, row missing | inert — order cleanup won |
| arm failed, row already terminal | do not overwrite |
| arm failed, row `scheduled`/`executing` | another actor owns it; queue nothing |
| arm failed, row unreadable | do not touch; the sweep is the backstop |
| lease lost, row `executing`/terminal/missing | legitimate loser, exit |
| lease failed, row still `scheduled` | re-queue and VERIFY (rule 3); terminalise `lease_write_failed` when the cap is spent |
| lease failed, read also failed | contained failure — **never** "the row was deleted" |
| cancel did not take ownership | **do not unschedule.** §4's re-validation is the backstop |

The gate on removing a job is `finalized` — *the tombstone holds a terminal status*
— and not `success`, which additionally requires the detail row. A delivery that is
genuinely terminal with a missing log row must still not keep a live job.

**A detail row is written only after ownership is won.** The recorders used to
write the row first, so a cancellation that lost to a worker left a `cancelled`
attempt row on a `sent` tombstone: a delivery log contradicting itself about an
email already in the customer's inbox. A lost transition is recorded as its own
diagnostic — a log line, at error level for a shortfall and notice level for a lost
race — because it is a fact about **that attempt**, not about the delivery.

This is the same defect class Prompt 2A fixed for `claim()`, where a suppressed
identity and a failed query both returned "did not claim". Fixing it in the
repository, once, is what stops a future call site reintroducing it; a source scan
(`WriteResultTest`) fails the build if one tries.

#### 8.2 `executing` is the lease

`ScheduledDelivery::run()` **begins** by attempting `scheduled → executing`. If
that fails it returns immediately **without sending**: another worker owns the
delivery, or it is already cancelled.

**⚠ THIS IS WHAT MAKES A RE-CLAIMED ACTION HARMLESS, AND THE CASE IT FIXES NEEDS
ONLY A PHP TIMEOUT.** A worker that dies *after* `wp_mail()` returned but *before*
the status write leaves the tombstone mid-flight and the action re-claimable.
Without a lease the next worker reads `scheduled`, re-validates successfully, and
**sends the customer a second email** — a duplicate produced by nothing more
exotic than `max_execution_time` on a busy host.

`executing` **retains the snapshot** — it is added to neither
`SNAPSHOT_RELEASING_STATUSES` nor to any releasing path — because the delivery is
still pending work, and a worker that loses its lease to a stale-lease sweep and
is later recovered must still know what it was going to send.

The lease records **when it was taken**, in a dedicated `lease_taken_at` column
rather than in `last_seen_at`. `last_seen_at` looks like it would do, and does
not: `claim()`'s `ON DUPLICATE KEY UPDATE` bumps it whenever the same trigger
fires again, so a busy order would silently extend its own lease and postpone the
recovery in §8.3 indefinitely. A column nothing else writes is the only one that
can mean "the lease was taken at". It is set on entry to `executing` and cleared
to `NULL` on every exit from it.

#### 8.3 A dead worker must not strand a delivery — the stale-lease sweep

A worker can die while `executing`, and then nothing would ever move the row
again. A daily maintenance action sweeps `executing` rows whose lease is older
than a bounded window and transitions them to **`unresolved`**, with the distinct
reason `lease_expired`.

**⚠ `unresolved`, NOT `failed`, AND THE DIFFERENCE IS THE POINT.** `failed`
asserts the message did not go out. Nobody knows that: the lease may have been
lost at any moment, including one microsecond after the mailer accepted the
message. `unresolved` already carries exactly the meaning required — *a send
happened and nothing was reported back* (ADR-0013 §6) — and it is the honest
answer. Recording `failed` here would also be the exact input that a future
resend feature would read as "retry this", which is how a stranded lease becomes
a duplicate email.

**The window is one hour, and the number is not arbitrary.** Against Action
Scheduler's own timings in the bundled WooCommerce 10.9.4 copy:

| Reference | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| queue-runner batch limit | 30 s | `ActionScheduler_Abstract_QueueRunner::get_time_limit()` |
| AS declares a *running* action dead | 300 s | `ActionScheduler_QueueCleaner::mark_failures()`, called with 10× the batch limit |
| AS unclaims a *pending* action | 300 s | `ActionScheduler_QueueCleaner::reset_timeouts()`, same multiplier |
| PHPMailer SMTP `Timeout` | 300 s | the longest single blocking call a `wp_mail()` can make |

One hour is **12× the largest of those**, and Action Scheduler has already given
up on the action long before we do. The window errs long deliberately, because
the two errors are not symmetric: sweeping **too early** marks a live send
`unresolved`, which is untrue and is the input a resend feature would act on;
sweeping **too late** only delays the log admitting it does not know. The first
can reach a customer. The second cannot.

**The sweep also recovers orphaned `scheduled` rows.** A row that is `scheduled`,
older than the same window, and has **no live job** in the queue is a delivery
nobody will ever run. The sweep re-schedules it for immediate execution — §4's
re-validation then decides whether it is still owed, so a late job cannot send
something stale — and, if the queue refuses it, transitions `scheduled →
cancelled` with reason `orphaned_no_job`. The age filter is what keeps this from
racing a delivery scheduled microseconds ago, and both halves of the sweep are
batch-capped **and cursor-paged** (§8.3a — the cap alone starved the orphan half)
so a store with a large backlog does a bounded amount of work per
day rather than one unbounded pass.

#### 8.3a The sweep is cursor-paged, and why it had to be (added Prompt 7B)

The orphan half selected `ORDER BY id ASC LIMIT 100` with **no cursor**, and a row
whose job exists simply `continue`d — advancing nothing and updating nothing. So a
store with more than `SWEEP_BATCH` aged pending deliveries re-examined **the same
first hundred every day, for ever**, and orphan #101 was never reached.

**That needs no database error, no third-party misbehaviour and no unusual
configuration** — only moderate volume plus a long delay, which is precisely the
feature this ADR exists to ship. It is the one defect in this ADR's history that
required nothing to go wrong.

**Cursor-paged, not windowed.** The alternative — stamping a "maintenance checked"
timestamp on healthy rows so they leave the candidate window — was rejected: it
writes to every healthy row on every pass, and it cannot reach an orphan sitting
behind a full page **in the same run**, which is exactly the case that failed. A
cursor costs one integer.

- Within a run, each half pages by `id > $after_id` across at most
  `SWEEP_MAX_PAGES` (10) pages of `SWEEP_BATCH` (100).
- The **orphan** half persists its cursor so a queue deeper than one run's budget
  makes progress across runs. The cursor is only ever a hint: nothing is lost if it
  is stale, missing or reset.
- The **lease** half needs no persisted cursor and has a different shape: every row
  it returns is transitioned out of `executing`, so the candidate set drains
  itself. Its within-run cursor exists only so that a row the sweep FAILED to
  recover cannot sit at the head of every page hiding the rows behind it.

> **⚠ SUPERSEDED IN PART BY §8.3b.** This section's original text claimed the cursor
> reset "when the candidate set is exhausted, so a row that became orphaned behind
> the cursor is reached on the next cycle rather than never", and allowed one wrap
> inside a run. **Both were true only of a quiet queue.** Under sustained inflow no
> page is ever short, the reset never fires, and the cursor climbs for ever. §8.3b
> replaces the reset with a bounded cycle and removes the in-run wrap; the paging
> above stands unchanged.

#### 8.3b The cursor runs inside a BOUNDED CYCLE, and that is the fairness guarantee (added Prompt 7C)

**The invariant, stated before the mechanism:**

> Every delivery that is eligible at the moment a sweep cycle begins is examined
> within `ceil(E / T)` maintenance runs, where `E` is the eligible count at cycle
> start and `T` is the per-run throughput — **regardless of how many new eligible
> rows arrive while the cycle is in progress**.

§8.3a satisfied this only when the queue was quiet. Its cursor reset when a page came
back short, and under sustained inflow a page is never short:

```
cursor 5000, orphan at id 100, >1,000 new eligible rows per day
day 1: 5001–6000   day 2: 6001–7000   day 3: 7001–8000  …
id 100 is never examined again; its email is permanently lost
```

**⚠ THIS DEFECT EXISTS ONLY BECAUSE THE PREVIOUS FIX EXISTS.** There was no cursor to
climb before 7B, and the 7B regression — 101 healthy rows starting at cursor 0 — proves
only that the sweep can pass page one *within a single run*. It cannot see a cursor
that never comes back.

**The mechanism: a cycle high-water mark.** A *cycle* is one ascending traversal of
one **frozen** candidate set, recorded in `extonify_wcep_sweep_cycle` as
`{cursor, high_water, cutoff}`:

- `high_water` — `MAX(id)` over the candidates, captured when the cycle opens. Page
  reads are bounded `id > cursor AND id <= high_water`, so rows arriving during a
  cycle join the **next** one instead of extending the current one.
- `cutoff` — the age predicate, captured at the same moment and reused for every page
  of the cycle. **This half is not decoration**: the id bound excludes rows *created*
  during the cycle, but not a row that already existed below the mark and merely
  became old enough mid-cycle.
- A cycle is complete when a page comes back short of the mark, or when the cursor
  reaches it. The cursor is then parked **on the mark**, and the next run recognises
  `cursor >= high_water` and opens a fresh cycle — with a fresh mark and a fresh
  cutoff, which is what lets a row orphaned behind the old cursor be reached.

**Why the set can only shrink, which is what makes the bound a proof rather than an
estimate.** Ids are monotonic, so anything created after the mark is above it.
`last_seen_at` on a `scheduled` row is only ever written forward — `arm_scheduled()`
and the §8.4 re-arm both stamp *now* — so a row above the frozen cutoff can never fall
back below it. Nothing can therefore enter an open cycle's candidate set; rows only
leave it, by being finalised, re-armed or recovered.

**`T = SWEEP_THROUGHPUT = SWEEP_BATCH × SWEEP_MAX_PAGES = 1,000` rows per run, and the
bound is `ceil(E / T)` runs.** Each run spends its whole page budget on one cycle and
examines that cycle's rows in strictly ascending id order, so after `n` runs the
lowest `min(n × T, E)` candidates have been examined.

**The in-run wrap is removed, and its removal is required rather than incidental.** A
wrap would hand a fresh cycle a partly-spent page budget, and `ceil(E / T)` would stop
being true of that cycle's first run. What the wrap existed to prevent — a whole run
wasted on a stale cursor past the end of the table — is now answered at the top of the
run instead, where opening the next cycle costs nothing that has already been spent.
The residue is one short page read on the run that closes a cycle whose last full page
happened to land exactly on the mark.

**What is NOT claimed.** A cycle's length grows with the backlog: if inflow genuinely
exceeds `T` for ever, `E` grows and so does `ceil(E / T)`. That is a throughput limit,
not a fairness defect, and it is stated in the invariant's own terms — every row is
still examined within a bound derived from the queue it is in, and no row is ever
overtaken indefinitely by newer ones.

**The lease half needs no mark, and this was re-examined under the same adversarial
inflow rather than re-asserted.** It holds, for a reason the orphan half cannot use:

1. every row the lease half reads is transitioned out of `executing`, so it leaves
   the candidate set as soon as it is recovered;
2. its cursor restarts at 0 every run, so each run reads the lowest surviving stale
   lease first;
3. a new stale lease has a higher id, and (2) means a higher id is never read before
   a lower one.

Inflow therefore cannot push an old row backwards, and `ceil(E / T)` holds without a
mark. **Its one starvation mode, stated rather than glossed:** a row whose recovery
*write* fails stays `executing` and stays a candidate, so `P` such rows cost `P` of
each run's `T`. That requires a database that accepts reads and refuses writes, it is
logged per row, and it is accepted residual risk — repairing it with a persisted
cursor would break property (2), which is the stronger guarantee.

**The bound, per run, stated in full:** at most 10 page reads per half (one indexed
SELECT each) covering at most 1,000 candidate rows per half; **plus one
`SELECT MAX(id)` per cycle opened, at most one per run**; plus one option read and one
option write for the cycle record; one `as_has_scheduled_action()` per orphan-half
candidate, short-circuiting on the first hit, so a healthy row costs exactly one
lookup and only an orphan pays the full `MAX_RESCHEDULES + 1`; one guarded UPDATE plus
one detail insert per row actually recovered. Nothing scales with the size of the
tombstone table.

#### 8.3c Recovery must be SELF-ARMING (added Prompt 7C)

> **Any mechanism whose absence makes another guarantee false must arm itself from
> the same runtime path that creates the work it protects, not from an event that may
> never fire.**

`Maintenance::ensure_scheduled()` was reachable from exactly two places: activation,
and `admin_init`. **An automatic plugin update runs neither** — WordPress does not
re-activate a plugin it updates in place — and a store driven by REST or WP-CLI can go
a long time without an admin request. So the plugin could schedule delayed deliveries
into a queue whose only recovery mechanism did not exist, and every guarantee in §8.3,
§8.3b and §8.4 rests on that action running.

**⚠ LIKE §8.3b, THIS BECAME ASKABLE ONLY BECAUSE THE PREVIOUS ROUND EXISTS.** There
was no maintenance action before Prompt 7A, so "is it guaranteed to be armed?" was not
a question anyone could have asked.

Two arming points, and both are needed:

- **`action_scheduler_init`** — fires on every request that loads Action Scheduler,
  front end, REST, cron and CLI alike, and is the point Action Scheduler itself
  declares its procedural API safe to use (`ActionScheduler::init()`, bundled 3.9.3).
  `action_scheduler_ensure_recurring_actions` is honoured too, as Action Scheduler's
  own daily invitation to re-register recurring work — but only as a second belt,
  because AS schedules the action that fires it from an **admin** request, which is
  the dependency this section exists to remove.
- **`ScheduledDelivery::schedule()`**, on every verified action id. Queue creation
  heals the queue's own recovery: the moment work exists that only the sweep can
  reach, the sweep is verified to exist.

**The cost.** `Maintenance::ensure_armed()` short-circuits on a request-scoped static,
then on an **autoloaded** option holding the last verification timestamp — the one
autoloaded option this plugin owns, because the alternative is a query on every
storefront page to save a few bytes in a blob WordPress has already fetched. Only once
per `VERIFY_INTERVAL_SECONDS` (the lease window, one hour) does a request additionally
pay one `as_next_scheduled_action()` and one option write. A request that queues fifty
deliveries pays the same as one that queues one.

**The stamp is written only when the answer is "armed"**: a failed arm must not buy an
hour of silence for a queue with no recovery behind it. Deactivation deletes it,
because the action has just been removed and the stamp would otherwise be a lie for up
to an hour across a deactivate/reactivate cycle.

#### 8.4 No exit leaves a scheduled delivery PERMANENTLY UNREACHABLE

> **⚠ THIS SECTION'S HEADING AND CONTRACT WERE CORRECTED IN PROMPT 7C, BECAUSE THE
> PREVIOUS ONE OVERSTATED WHAT THE CODE DOES.** Prompt 7A's report claimed "no `run()`
> branch leaves `scheduled` without a live job". `reschedule_transient()` deliberately
> does exactly that in three cases — the re-schedule cap is spent, terminalisation also
> fails, or Action Scheduler refuses the replacement — and its own log lines say so.
> That is a defensible strategy; it was not the guarantee that was claimed. **A gate
> that overstates is worse than one that admits a bound**, because the overstatement is
> what stops anyone checking the thing it depends on. The corrected contract is below,
> and it is asserted as a conjunction rather than as the absence of such exits.

Every branch of `run()` does exactly one of three things:

1. reach a terminal state; or
2. transition to a terminal state with a truthful, distinct reason; or
3. leave the tombstone `scheduled` and **prove a replacement job exists** —
   re-schedule, verify the returned action id, and cap the re-schedules so it
   cannot loop.

**and no branch leaves a scheduled delivery permanently unreachable.** A branch may
leave one temporarily without its delivery job **only when both of these hold**:

- a verified recurring maintenance action exists (§8.3c), **and**
- the fairness bound of §8.3b gives it a stated recovery time — examined within
  `ceil(E / T)` maintenance runs of the cycle that follows.

Those two halves are exactly what §8.3b and §8.3c exist to make true, and they are
asserted together: for every jobless-but-`scheduled` exit, that the maintenance action
is armed **and** that the sweep reaches the row within the declared bound. Neither
half is worth anything alone — a bounded sweep that is not scheduled never runs, and a
scheduled sweep that starves never arrives.

**(3) applies to genuinely transient conditions only, and there is exactly one:
`! Migrator::is_operational()`, which is true while a plugin update is in
flight.** That branch cannot use (2) even in principle — recording a terminal
state means writing to a table that by hypothesis is not there. Everything else
takes (2), with its own reason: `schema_unavailable`, `action_arguments_mismatch`,
`email_class_unavailable`, `execution_inoperative`.

**The jobless-but-scheduled exits, enumerated.** All three are inside
`reschedule_transient()`, all three log the fact, and all three are covered by the
conjunction above:

| Exit | Condition | Asserted against the conjunction |
|---|---|---|
| cap spent, schema still down | `attempt > MAX_RESCHEDULES` and `! Migrator::is_operational( true )` | yes |
| queue refused the replacement | `schedule()` returned neither `scheduled` nor `already_pending` | yes |
| cap spent, terminalisation lost or failed | the forced re-check passes but `cancel()` does not finalise | **reachable only through a write failure** — a *lost* cancellation does not reach it, because §8.1a's read then reports the row terminal and the branch returns early. It joins the same tail as the other two and the same conjunction covers it |

The re-schedule count rides in the **action arguments** rather than on the
tombstone, for the same reason: the tombstone is unreachable in the only branch
that needs the count. `args()` therefore carries an `attempt`, and
`unschedule()` sweeps the bounded attempt range so that cancelling a delivery
still cancels **every** job that delivery owns. The initial `schedule()` checks
attempt 0 alone, which is exact rather than approximate: a re-scheduled job only
ever exists after a run, and a run only happens after the tombstone left
`claimed`, so a second trigger reaching `schedule()` was already suppressed by
the claim.

#### 8.5 Scheduling is inside a containment boundary

The whole post-claim queue operation is wrapped, the way `Orchestrator::attempt()`
wraps the immediate path. On a `Throwable`: if a job exists the tombstone keeps
`scheduled` and the diagnostic shortfall is recorded; if none does, the tombstone
transitions to a terminal state and the snapshot is released. **The exception
never escapes into the WooCommerce event** — a queue backend having a bad day
must not break the merchant's order status change.

#### 8.6 Lifecycle events finalise, they do not merely unschedule

**Deactivation.** For every `scheduled` tombstone: transition `scheduled →
cancelled` with reason `plugin_deactivated` (which releases the snapshot),
**then** unschedule that delivery's exact job, and only then run the hook-wide
sweep as a backstop. The order matters and is the same one §5 argues for: if the
process dies between the two halves, a surviving job finds a terminal tombstone
and stops, whereas the opposite order leaves a `scheduled` tombstone owed to
nobody.

**Uninstall.** The Action Scheduler cleanup moves **above** the retain-data early
return, and pending tombstones are finalised and their snapshots released on that
path too. Only the table drops stay conditional on the option. A site owner who
keeps their data is not asking to keep a queue of actions whose hook no longer
exists.

**Order deletion.** Each delivery's exact action is unscheduled **before** its
tombstones are deleted, so no action outlives the row it points at. An unschedule
failure is logged and cleanup proceeds — once the tombstone is gone the job is
harmless, because `run()` finds no row and stops.

#### 8.7 §1a still reads correctly, and now it has to cover bulk

§1a says a cancelled delayed delivery has **permanently consumed its identity**.
Deactivation and uninstall now cancel in bulk, so that sentence acquires a
consequence worth stating rather than discovering: **deactivating the plugin
permanently cancels every in-flight delayed delivery, and reactivating does not
bring them back.** The tombstones remain, terminal, with `plugin_deactivated`
recorded against them.

That is the correct behaviour and it follows directly from §1a's own argument —
releasing identities on cancellation would mean a plugin toggled off and on
re-delivers to every order still inside its delay window, which is a far worse
failure than a delayed email a merchant interrupted by deactivating the plugin.
The remedy is the one §1a already names: a manual resend through the linked
attempt rows.

#### 8.8 THE SHUTDOWN POLICY, for BOTH pending states (added Prompt 7B)

§8.6 covered `scheduled`. It did not cover `executing`, which appeared in no
lifecycle path at all — only in a comment explaining that a running delivery is not
cancelled out from under its worker. **That reasoning is right during normal
running and wrong during shutdown.** A merchant who deactivated mid-flight left a
row `executing` with its snapshot retained, and deactivation also removes the
maintenance action — so the §8.3 sweep that would have recovered it an hour later
was gone too. Nothing could ever move that row again.

| Event | `scheduled` | `executing` | Snapshot | Jobs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deactivation | → `cancelled` (`plugin_deactivated`) | → `unresolved` (`lease_interrupted_by_shutdown`) | released | removed **per delivery, after it is terminal**; hook-wide sweep only if nothing is left pending |
| Uninstall, data retained | → `cancelled` | → `unresolved` | released | as above, in dependency-free SQL |
| Uninstall, data removed | → `cancelled`, then the table is dropped | → `unresolved`, then dropped | gone with the table | removed once nothing is pending |
| Order deletion | tombstone deleted | tombstone deleted | gone with the row | that delivery's exact job removed **first** (§8.6) |
| Schema unavailable | untouched | untouched | untouched | **nothing removed** — every job stays queued |

**`unresolved`, not `cancelled` and not `failed`, for an interrupted lease.** The
plugin cannot know whether the running worker sent before the shutdown. `cancelled`
and `failed` both assert something about a customer's inbox that nothing here is in
a position to assert, and `failed` is specifically the input a future resend feature
would read as "retry this" (§8.3). The transition is the existing `executing →
unresolved` edge; only the recorded reason differs, because *"the worker vanished"*
and *"the site owner shut the plugin down"* are different answers to **why didn't
this send?**

**The hook-wide unschedule has a precondition.**
`as_unschedule_all_actions( $hook, null, $group )` is a sweep with no tombstone in
its hand: it removes every job whether or not the row behind it was finalised.
`cancel_all_pending()` returns early when the schema is unreadable, and the sweep
below it used to run regardless — producing exactly the stranded state this ADR
exists to eliminate, from the code written to prevent it. **It now runs only when
every pending tombstone was finalised**, and the condition is logged loudly
otherwise. A job left queued for an inactive plugin does nothing at all — its hook
has no handler — so leaving it is strictly the safer failure; a tombstone with no
job is not recoverable.

**§1a and §8.7 extend unchanged to the `executing` half.** An interrupted lease has
consumed its identity too, and reactivating does not resume it.

**One honest limitation, recorded rather than glossed.** `uninstall.php` is
dependency-free by design (no autoloader, no WooCommerce), and a detail row is
allocated inside a transaction holding the parent lock
(`DeliveryDetailRepository::record_attempt()`). The uninstall path therefore records
the terminal **status** and releases the snapshot, but writes no per-delivery reason
row. That is why there is no `plugin_uninstalled` reason code: a constant nothing
can produce is drift, and gate 9 forbids it.

#### 8.9 ONE recorder writes its detail rows before taking ownership, on purpose (added Prompt 7C)

Prompt 7B moved four recorders — cancellation, arm failure, expired lease, scheduled
failure — to **win the guarded transition first, then write the detail row**. For
those four that is the only defensible order: their detail row *is* the outcome, so
writing one before winning the transition claims an outcome belonging to whoever did
win. The shared **send** recorder (`write_attempt_rows()`) still writes its recipient
rows first, and it stays that way.

**The difference is what the row is evidence of.** By the time the send recorder runs,
`wp_mail()` has already been called and a message either is or is not in a customer's
inbox. That fact is not conditional on winning a transition, and suppressing it on a
lost race would delete the only record of a real email — including the row the privacy
exporter and eraser find by recipient.

**And reordering does not fix the state it appears to fix.** The state in question — a
`unresolved` tombstone carrying a `sent` detail row — is produced by a *concurrent*
finaliser (deactivation, the §8.3 sweep), not by the order of these two writes.
Finalise-first would lose the same race, would still have to write the same evidence
afterwards, and would additionally risk a **terminal tombstone with no detail row at
all** if the process died between the two — which is the ADR-0012 §3 failure, traded
for an audit-tidiness gain that does not materialise.

**What the pair actually needed was to be self-explaining, and that is the fix.** A
lost race leaves `finalize()` reporting `terminal` true — because the row really is
terminal and somebody really does own it — so `verify()` computed success and logged
**nothing**. `note_lost_attempt_ownership()` now records it: the message went out, and
the outcome column belongs to whoever won the transition. Immediate-path callers pass
`$from = null` and are skipped, because that path has no second actor.

## Consequences

- A merchant can delay a delivery, and the message they get is the message they
  authored when it was scheduled, addressed to the customer as that customer is at
  send time.
- A cancelled delayed delivery consumes its identity permanently (§1a). This is the
  one consequence most likely to be misread as a defect, and it is recorded here so
  the reading is settled once.
- The durable tombstone grows by one snapshot per **pending** delivery, released at
  terminal state — so the storage cost tracks queue depth, not history.
- A privacy erasure during the delay does not break the queue: the snapshot is not
  in the purgeable store. The message itself resolves fresh, so an anonymised order
  yields no deliverable recipient and the delivery records a skip with its reason —
  the existing mechanism, reached honestly, rather than a new policy invented here.
- Two cancellation paths mean a disabled rule stops mail immediately **and** a job
  that escaped the sweep still declines to send.
- The scheduler is not trusted for uniqueness, and the ADR-0004 claim is not asked
  to be timely. Each does the job it is actually capable of.
